## One-line Summary
Review of nanobiotechnology strategies — molecular modifications, self-assembly, and nanoformulations — for enhancing mRNA stability, with emphasis on the structure-function relationships that determine degradation resistance and shelf-life.

## 2. Key Contributions
- First review to focus specifically on how nanomaterials and nanostructures contribute to mRNA stability (as distinct from delivery)
- Introduces a structure-function framework for understanding how different nanobiotechnology approaches counteract specific mRNA destabilization mechanisms
- Covers in-line hydrolysis, base oxidation, RNase activity, decapping, deadenylation as key degradation pathways
- Reviews mRNA sequence design, self-assembly into nanostructures, and nano-encapsulation in lipids/polymers
- Addresses manufacturing considerations (freezing, lyophilization) for long-term formulation stability

## 3. Methodology and Architecture
- Narrative review organized by: (1) destabilization factors, (2) molecular strategies, (3) nano-encapsulation strategies, (4) manufacturing considerations
- mRNA structure: 5' cap, 5' UTR, coding sequence, 3' UTR, poly(A) tail — each regulatory element essential to stability
- In-line hydrolysis mechanism: 2'-OH nucleophilic attack on adjacent phosphate, forming 2',3'-cyclic phosphate; accelerated by high temperature, RNases, alkaline pH
- Oxidative degradation: ROS oxidize guanosine to 8-oxoguanosine, reducing peptide bond formation, triggering no-go decay
- Cellular pathways: decapping (DCP1/DCP2 recognize 5' cap m7GpppG), deadenylation (Caf1-Ccr4, Pan2-Pan3), miRNA-mediated repression
- Nanoencapsulation condensation via electrostatic and pi-pi stacking interactions protects mRNA from enzymatic and storage degradation

## 4. Key Results and Benchmarks
- Pfizer-BioNTech vaccine: −80°C storage for 9 months; 4°C for ~2.5 months
- Moderna vaccine: −20°C for 6 months; 4°C for 1 month
- mRNA is more susceptible to degradation than double-stranded DNA due to single-stranded nature and 2'-OH
- Chemical modifications reviewed: N1-methyl-pseudouridine (m1Ψ), 2'-OMe, 2'-F — reduce RNase susceptibility and immunogenicity
- Self-complementary mRNA sequences can form double-stranded regions resisting single-strand-specific RNases
- LNP encapsulation condenses mRNA via electrostatic interaction, limiting water access and protecting against enzymatic degradation
- Polymer nanoparticles (PLGA, PEI-based): additional stability in dry formulations
- Lyophilization with trehalose/sucrose cryoprotectants preserves mRNA-LNP physical integrity and activity

## 5. Limitations and Future Work
- Most nanoformulation stability data are from in vitro or accelerated aging studies; long-term real-world data limited
- Structure-function relationships for mRNA nanostructures in complex biological environments remain poorly understood
- Balance between stability and translatability: over-structured mRNA can impair ribosome access
- Future: rational sequence design algorithms, self-stabilizing RNA architectures, stable solid-state formulations for cold-chain-free distribution

## 7. Glossary
- **In-line hydrolysis**: Intramolecular cleavage of mRNA phosphodiester bond by 2'-OH; primary spontaneous degradation mechanism
- **m1Ψ (N1-methyl-pseudouridine)**: Modified uridine used in COVID-19 vaccines; reduces immunogenicity, improves stability
- **LNP (Lipid Nanoparticle)**: Self-assembled nanocarrier composed of ionizable lipid, helper lipid, PEG-lipid, and cholesterol; primary mRNA delivery vehicle
- **Decapping**: Enzymatic removal of 5' cap by DCP1/DCP2; initiates mRNA decay
- **Deadenylation**: Shortening of 3' poly(A) tail by Caf1-Ccr4 or Pan2-Pan3 complexes; triggers mRNA decay
- **No-go decay**: mRNA quality control pathway triggered by stalled ribosomes; involves endonuclease cleavage
- **8-oxoguanosine**: Oxidized form of guanosine; reduces translation fidelity and triggers no-go decay
- **Lyophilization**: Freeze-drying process; removes water by sublimation; stabilizes pharmaceutical formulations
